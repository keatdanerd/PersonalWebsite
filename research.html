Research

Research

I like problems that need more than one field to solve. My work has moved from bacteria and enzymes, through organometallic synthesis and laser spectroscopy, to quantum sensing, and each step taught me a new way to ask a question.

Current

Quantum Sensing with Molecular Spins and 2D Materials

The University of Texas at Austin · 2026–present
Co-advised by Prof. Sean Roberts (Chemistry) and Prof. Xiaoqin Elaine Li (Physics)

Quantum sensors use fragile quantum states, such as the spin of an electron, as extraordinarily sensitive probes of their surroundings. My Ph.D. research sits at the intersection of chemistry and materials: I am interested in how molecules can serve as optically addressable spin sensors, and how they can be paired with two-dimensional materials to probe physics at the nanoscale.

I am helping build a new optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) setup, and I am training across both groups, in time-resolved and ultrafast spectroscopy in the Roberts lab and in 2D-material sample preparation and device fabrication in the Li lab.

  • ODMR
  • Molecular spin
  • Ultrafast spectroscopy
  • TCSPC
  • 2D materials
  • Device fabrication

Laser Spectroscopy of Reactive Carbon Radicals

California Institute of Technology · Summer 2025
Southern California Edison WAVE Fellow · Prof. Mitchio Okumura · Mentor: Kristen Roehling

Resonance-stabilized radicals are thought to be stepping stones toward polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), the large carbon molecules found in combustion and in interstellar space. Working in experimental astrochemistry, I studied the excited states of these radicals by combining spectroscopy with computation.

I also designed an experiment to follow carbon radical recombination in real time using pulsed laser photolysis with cavity ring-down spectroscopy, a technique sensitive enough to detect trace amounts of short-lived species.

  • Cavity ring-down spectroscopy
  • Pulsed laser photolysis
  • Optics
  • Computation
  • Astrochemistry

Osmium Organometallics as Hydrogenase Mimics

Abilene Christian University · 2025–2026
Perry C. Reeves Research Fellow · Profs. Greg and Cynthia Powell

Hydrogenase enzymes make and split hydrogen using cheap, abundant metals, and chemists build small “biomimic” molecules to learn how they do it. I synthesized new osmium organometallic compounds as hydrogenase mimics and characterized their structures by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

I presented this work at the Joint SERMACS/SWRM American Chemical Society Regional Meeting in Orlando, Florida (October 2025).

  • Organometallic synthesis
  • X-ray crystallography
  • Bioinorganic chemistry

Protein Assembly in the Mevalonate Pathway

Abilene Christian University · 2023–2025
Research Assistant, Biochemistry and Microbiology · Profs. Autumn Sutherlin and Jennifer Hennigan

The mevalonate pathway builds the precursors to cholesterol, steroid hormones, and thousands of natural products. I advanced the study of the multimeric (multi-subunit) states of a fusion protein from this pathway, using plasmid vector manipulation and protein expression to produce and examine it.

  • Molecular cloning
  • Protein expression
  • Gel electrophoresis

Quorum Sensing in Serratia marcescens

Abilene Christian University · 2023–2025
Research Assistant, Biochemistry and Microbiology · Profs. Autumn Sutherlin and Jennifer Hennigan

Bacteria coordinate their behavior by “counting” one another through chemical signals, a process called quorum sensing. I studied quorum-sensing inhibition and the biosynthetic pathway of prodigiosin, the vivid red pigment that S. marcescens produces under quorum-sensing control.

  • Microbiology
  • Natural product biosynthesis
  • Quorum sensing
